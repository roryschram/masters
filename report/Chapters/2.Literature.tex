\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:Literature}
This chapter aims to add context to the entire project by exploring the existing literature on RADAR systems, 
the OFDM modulation scheme, and the combination of the two within a single system in order to achieve the objective 
of object classification. In order to get to a level where the topic of object classification can be discussed, one 
must first delve into the fundamental operation of RADAR and OFDM based systems. Once one has a strong understanding 
of these basics, the combination of the two can be discussed in regard to object classification. 



\section{RADAR}
\subsection{Brief RADAR History}
The basic idea of RADAR was experimented with in the classical electromagnetic radiation experiments of the 1880s 
performed by Heinrich Hertz \cite{skolnik_radar_2024}. These experiments set out to prove the previous theoretical 
work done by James Clerk Maxwell. Maxwell came up with a formula that described electromagnetic fields, and he 
hypothesized that this formula would apply to both light and radio waves since they were both examples of electromagnetic 
radiation, just at very different frequencies. The work done by Maxwell led to the discovery that electromagnetic waves 
can be reflected by metallic objects and refracted by a dielectric medium, just like light can \cite{skolnik_radar_2024}. 
This work did not go unnoticed, and a patent for an "an obstacle detector and ship navigation device" was issued in 1904 
to Christian Hülsmeyer, a German engineer \cite{skolnik_radar_2024}. Work on RADAR then slowed down due to a lack of 
interest from governmental authorities until the early 1930s, when the development of long-range bombers prompted the 
major countries of the world to develop technologies which could detect these hostile aircraft.



\subsection{RADAR Basics}
The fundamental operation of a simple single pulse RADAR system relies on three physical fundamental properties of the universe 
\cite{christian_wolff_physical_2024} which are listed below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reflection of electromagnetic waves}: This rule describes how a radio wave will be reflected 
    back towards a transmitter of said radio wave if the object is placed directly in front of the transmitter. 
    This reflection indicates that the object is in front of the transmitter.
    \item \textbf{Electromagnetic energy travels through air at a constant speed}: This rule describes how the 
    electromagnetic waves transmitted by an antenna propagate through the transmission medium, usually air for our 
    earth's use cases, at roughly the speed of light, $c$. This velocity $c$ equates to 299,792,458 $m.s^{-1}$ 
    \item \textbf{Electromagnetic energy normally travels through space in a straight line}: This describes how the 
    energy transmitted by an antenna in a radar system propagates through space in a straight line. This is important 
    because it means that the energy of the RADAR system can be directed in a specific direction by the antenna system, 
    which means that the reflecting object's direction (azimuth and elevation) can be determined.
\end{enumerate}

Using the above-mentioned physical properties, the distance, azimuth, and elevation of the object that is being looked at 
by the radar system can be determined.

Since RADAR utilizes the electromagnetic spectrum, there are distinct advantages concerning the use of RADAR 
systems \cite{christian_wolff_physical_2024}. The main advantage is that, unlike humans, RADAR systems can 
operate day and night, 24/7, over far distances, in good or bad weather. Compared to human eyes, this is much better. 
The above example of a RADAR system is that of a simple single pulse RADAR. In the next section I want to outline the 
other type of RADAR system that I might use in my OFDM system, a pulsed RADAR system.


\section{OFDM}
\subsection{Brief OFDM History}
The idea of OFDM transmission techniques has been around for many years, however, it was never implemented in communication channels until the appearance of mass market applications coincided with the availability of efficient software and electronic technologies \cite{weinstein_history_2009}. Before OFDM however, there was \gls{fdm}.

\subsubsection{FDM}
FDM system innovation was built out of necessity by telegraph companies hoping to increase the amount of users that could utilize their transmission lines so as to increase profits from their systems \cite{weinstein_history_2009}. Transmission companies needed a method to have multiple messages being sent on the same transmission line at one time. Other methods had been used such as \gls{tdm}, and \gls{tdma}. These methods were however limited by burst speed which was itself limited by inter-symbol interference. Around the 1920s, FDM became the main carrier system for telephone companies. The first major company to deploy the system was \gls{att}.






\subsection{OFDM Basics}
\gls{ofdm} is a method of data transmission that breaks information into bit chunks that are sent 
in parallel over multiple channels \cite{wright_what_nodate}. The OFDM transmission scheme builds 
upon FDM where multiple channels are created over a specific bandwidth, and bits of information are 
sent on each channel in parallel. The main difference between FDM and OFDM is therefore the orthogonality of 
the OFDM signal. Pictured below in figure \ref{fig:ofdmandfdmwaveforms} is an OFDM, and FDM based waveform. 
As can be seen, all the peaks of the OFDM waveform have zero interference with any other part of the signal, 
whereas, with the FDM waveform, the peaks have significant interference from the other channels since the 
channels are not spaced orthogonally in regard to each other.
